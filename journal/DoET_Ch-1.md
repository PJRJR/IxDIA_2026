# Reading Discussion 1: Chapter 1 of DOET

<p> Although I’ve read this book before during my undergrad (*+ listened to the audiobook on too many flights*), it’s really helpful for me to reread and reflect on it again to hopefully catch any details that get lost in the [memory] sauce. Two ideas stood out most: *perceivable signifiers* and natural mapping.</p>

## Signifiers That Fail to Signify

<p>Norman defines a signifier as any perceivable indicator that communicates appropriate behavior to a person, and he is direct about the consequence of when that perceivability is missing: a signifier that cannot be seen or understood fails (p. 18-19). Reading that line, I thought immediately of two devices I use almost daily.</p>

<ul>
<li>My reMarkable e-ink tablet handles page navigation through swipe gestures, but nothing on the screen tells you how many fingers to use or which direction produces which result. The same is true of Procreate on my iPad. Both interfaces keep a menu anchored to one side, but a meaningful amount of actions only exist behind gestures that are easily forgettable - which I semi-often have to look up on the internet just to re-learn how to copy/paste, adjust layer opacity, etc. In Norman's terms, an affordance exists (the gesture works once you know it), but the signifier does not, so the affordance is functionally invisible. It’s a good reminder for me that a feature is only as real as the cue that reveals it.</li>
</ul>

## Natural Mapping in My Own Living Room

<p>The idea of “natural” mapping landed differently, because I realized I already live inside a good example of it. Norman describes natural mapping as using spatial analogies from the physical world to make controls intuitive without instruction (p. 22).</p>

<p>The light switch panel in my apartment controls my kitchen, hallway, and living room, and the switches are arranged left to right in the same order the rooms actually sit in space: living room on the left, hallway in the middle, kitchen on the right. I never had to learn this mapping. I understood it the first time I looked at the panel because the panel mirrors the room.</p>
<ul>
<li>This also reminded me of axis inversion in video games. I’ve always preferred inverted Y-axis controls, especially in games where I am piloting something that flies, but never found inverted X-axis settings useful (or even usable), maybe because I don’t play a lot of boat-driving games full of tillers. My best guess for why is that pulling back to “climb” echoes a real physical motion (tilting your head or body back to look up). If true, then Y-inversion is really just a different flavor of natural mapping.</li>
</ul>

## Closing Thought

<p>What ties both examples together is that neither failure nor success required new technology. The reMarkable and Procreate problems could be fixed with a one-line visual hint (the former having a hint if it detects you’re using too little amount of fingers or incomplete swipe). My light switch panel works simply because someone installed it in the order the rooms already exist. Norman's argument that bad design is a failure of communication rather than a failure of the user feels validating every time find myself searching the web for a gesture that could (should?) have been visible on the screen in the first place.</p>
