# Reading Discussion 2: Chapter 2 of DOET

<p>Chapter 2 shifted from <em>what</em> makes design fail to <em>why</em> people respond to failure the way they do. Two ideas stood out the most to me: <strong>the validation of the user</strong> and <strong>the three levels of processing.</strong></p>

## Blaming Ourselves for Bad Design

<p>Just like the author, I semi-often observe people (as well as myself) blaming themselves when they can’t figure out how to use a tool/technology, like my mother trying to send an email with an attachment; this repeated “failure” can falsely teach them they're simply incapable, which he calls <strong>learned helplessness</strong>. People outside of the tech industry often say to me "I'm just not a tech person," which frustrates me since I can often notice plenty of usability issues with whatever tool is causing this self-deprecation to emerge.</p>

<p>It also hit close to home because of my interest in language learning. Stephen Krashen, a linguist known for his theories of second language acquisition, argues through his <strong>affective filter hypothesis</strong> (essentially <strong>self-efficacy</strong>) that anxiety and low self-confidence block learning. A learner who says "I'm just not good at languages" is doing the same thing as someone who says "I'm just bad with computers." and is subconsciously setting themselves up for failure; similar to math-phobia, as mentioned by Don Norman, which I experience myself to this day in the form of code-phobia.</p>

## Reflective Design Is What Lasts

<p>Lastly, I also enjoyed reflecting on Norman's <strong>three levels of processing</strong>: <strong>Visceral</strong>: the automatic “lizard-brain” reaction, <strong>Behavioral</strong>: learned action, and <strong>Reflective</strong>: conscious thought.</p>

<p>What I appreciated most was his emphasis on the reflective level for designers since it's the most tied to memory. In the case of my master’s program and career development as a designer, keeping an awareness of this really helps shift the question(s) from "does it work in the moment/do they like it right away?" to "what will someone remember about using this?" And as a full-circle, the last thing I’d want users to remember is a self-deprecating experience and feeling helpless; in this case the goal to leave the user empowered and delighted is all the more reason to dig deeper into their goals, motivations, and help design positive, lasting experiences.</p>
